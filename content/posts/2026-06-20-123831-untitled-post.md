<div># AF Fine Adjustment: Fixing the Camera That Lies About Focus<br><br>You line up the shot, the focus point lights up green, the camera tells you it&#x27;s sharp. You press the shutter, feel good about it, and move on. Then you get home, zoom in, and the eyes are soft — the real plane of focus has landed on an eyelash, an ear, the collar, somewhere just in front of or just behind where you aimed. The camera said yes. The camera was wrong.<br><br>That gap between *confirmed* and *actually sharp* is what AF Fine Adjustment exists to close. It&#x27;s one of the more misunderstood features buried in modern DSLR menus, partly because it sounds like a fussy pro-only tool and partly because the camera never advertises that it might need calibrating in the first place. This article explains what the feature does, why the problem happens, and how to dial it in without losing an afternoon.<br><br>## What&#x27;s actually going wrong<br><br>Most DSLRs focus using a dedicated **phase-detection autofocus sensor** sitting in the bottom of the mirror box. Light bounces off the main mirror, through a secondary mirror, and down onto this sensor, which measures focus by comparing two views of the scene. It&#x27;s fast and it works well — but it&#x27;s a separate optical path from the one that actually exposes your image at the sensor plane.<br><br>Because it&#x27;s a separate path, it has to be calibrated so that &quot;in focus according to the AF sensor&quot; matches &quot;in focus at the imaging sensor.&quot; Camera makers do this at the factory, but the calibration is never perfect, and it can drift. Three things commonly throw it off:<br><br>- **Sample variation between camera and lens.** Your specific body and your specific lens each have tiny tolerances. Most of the time they cancel out. Sometimes they stack up in the same direction, and that particular pairing consistently misses.<br>- **Manual lenses on a body calibrated for autofocus glass.** This is the big one for adapted and vintage lenses. The focus-confirmation light still fires when you focus by hand, but it&#x27;s driven by that same AF sensor, calibrated for the maker&#x27;s own lenses. With a manual lens it can confirm a hair early or a hair late, every time.<br>- **Shooting wide open.** None of this matters at f/8, where depth of field is generous enough to hide a small error. At f/1.4 or f/1.8 the in-focus zone is millimetres deep, and the same small offset that was invisible stopped down becomes an obvious miss.<br><br>The tell-tale sign that fine adjustment can help is **consistency**. If your misses always land in front of the target, that&#x27;s *front-focus*. If they always land behind, that&#x27;s *back-focus*. A consistent, repeatable offset is exactly what this feature corrects. Random misses that land in front one frame and behind the next are a different problem — usually focus technique, subject movement, or a confirmation that&#x27;s simply at the edge of its tolerance — and fine adjustment won&#x27;t save you there.<br><br>## Confirming the problem before you touch anything<br><br>Don&#x27;t adjust on a hunch. Spend ten minutes proving the error is real, consistent, and in a known direction. The classic test:<br><br>1. Lay a ruler, a tape measure, or a row of objects on a table, running away from you at roughly **45 degrees**.<br>2. Put the camera on a tripod if you have one — it removes you as a variable. Handheld works, but be disciplined.<br>3. Open the lens to its **widest aperture**, where the error shows most clearly.<br>4. Pick one specific mark — say, the 20cm line — and focus on it. With autofocus, put the point there and let it lock. With a manual lens, focus by eye and/or to the confirmation light on that exact spot.<br>5. Shoot several frames, refocusing from scratch each time.<br>6. Zoom in on the results. Where is the genuinely sharp line? On your target, or in front, or behind?<br><br>If the sharp plane sits consistently in front of your target across multiple frames, you have consistent front-focus and you&#x27;ll correct *toward the back*. Consistently behind means back-focus, corrected *toward the front*. If it&#x27;s all over the place, stop here — fine adjustment is the wrong tool.<br><br>## Dialling it in<br><br>The feature lives in the custom settings menu. Names and exact locations vary by brand and model, but the logic is identical everywhere:<br><br>- **Pentax:** *AF Fine Adjustment*, in the Custom (C) menus.<br>- **Canon:** *AF Microadjustment*, in the autofocus custom menus.<br>- **Nikon:** *AF Fine-tune*, in the setup menu.<br><br>You&#x27;ll generally get two modes:<br><br>- **Apply All / Adjust All** — a single global correction applied to every lens you mount.<br>- **Apply One / per-lens** — a correction stored individually for each lens, recalled automatically when that lens is recognised.<br><br>Per-lens is the better choice when it works, because each lens can have its own offset and they may differ. But it relies on the lens reporting an electronic identity to the body. **Fully manual and adapted lenses usually can&#x27;t be stored individually**, so for those you fall back on the global *Apply All* setting — with the important consequence that it shifts the calibration for *everything*, including your autofocus lenses. If you swap constantly between adapted glass and native AF lenses, keep that in mind: a global tweak tuned for the manual lens may nudge your AF lenses off.<br><br>The adjustment itself is a scale, commonly **−10 to +10** with zero in the middle. The direction convention is the part everyone second-guesses, so anchor it like this:<br><br>- Shots **front-focus** (sharp plane too close to you) → move toward **+** to push focus back.<br>- Shots **back-focus** (sharp plane too far) → move toward **−** to pull focus forward.<br><br>Change a few steps, not the whole range. Re-shoot your 45-degree test. Look again. Repeat. You&#x27;re hunting for the setting where the sharp plane sits right on the line the camera confirmed. It&#x27;s genuinely iterative — expect three or four rounds, not a single lucky guess. When you get close, fine-tune one step at a time.<br><br>## On the Pentax K-50 specifically<br><br>Here&#x27;s the concrete version for the K-50 and the lenses in question — the **Cosinon-S 50mm f/1.8** (K-mount, mounts natively) and the **Super-Takumar 50mm f/1.4** (M42, on an adapter). Both are fully manual, so the camera focuses nothing itself; you turn the ring by hand and the K-50&#x27;s green focus-confirmation dot lights up when its AF sensor thinks you&#x27;ve nailed it. That dot is the thing that&#x27;s been lying — confirming focus that lands a little in front of or behind the real subject when you&#x27;re shooting wide open.<br><br>The setting on the K-50 is here:<br><br>1. Press **MENU** and go to the **Custom Setting** menus.<br>2. Find **C3**, item **21 — AF Fine Adjustment**.<br>3. Set it to **On / Apply All**. (Apply All is the right choice, not per-lens: your Cosinon and Takumar are manual and don&#x27;t report an electronic identity, so the camera can&#x27;t store a separate value for each. The global correction is what affects them.)<br>4. On the −10 to +10 scale, move it a few steps **toward the back** — i.e. the **plus (+)** direction.<br><br>Why the back/plus direction for your situation: you&#x27;ve been seeing the focus land slightly in front of where the dot confirmed (front-focus). Pushing the adjustment toward + shifts the confirmation point rearward so the sharp plane moves back onto your subject. Start with something like **+3 or +4**, then test and refine.<br><br>Then re-shoot the 45-degree ruler test wide open (f/1.8 on the Cosinon, f/1.4 on the Takumar) and check where the sharp line actually falls. Nudge one or two steps at a time until the confirmed point and the real sharp plane agree.<br><br>Two K-50 caveats worth holding onto:<br><br>- Because it&#x27;s a **global** setting, it nudges *every* lens — including any autofocus lens you mount later. If you tune it for the manual fifties and then put an AF lens on, remember the offset is still applied. Reset to 0 when you go back to AF glass if you notice it drifting.<br>- The adjustment fixes a **consistent** front-focus. The Takumar has the extra variable of adapter thickness, which can also affect where focus lands and can cost you infinity focus outright — so if the Takumar still misses after you&#x27;ve calibrated, suspect the adapter, not the camera.<br><br>For absolute certainty wide open with either lens, the fallback never changes: switch to **Live View, magnify to 100%, and focus by eye** on the subject. That reads focus straight off the imaging sensor and ignores the AF dot entirely, so there&#x27;s nothing to calibrate.<br><br>## When the menu isn&#x27;t the answer<br><br>Fine adjustment is a fix for a *consistent* offset, and it has real limits. Two situations call for a different approach.<br><br>**Random misses.** If your test frames scatter — in front, then behind, then on target — there&#x27;s no single number that corrects them. This is often technique (focus-and-recompose shifting the plane), a subject that moved, or a confirmation light that&#x27;s simply imprecise with your lens. The cure is better focusing, not menu diving.<br><br>**Manual lenses you want dead-on, wide open.** The most reliable answer here bypasses the phase-detect sensor entirely: **use Live View, magnify the image to 100%, and focus by eye on the actual feed off the imaging sensor.** Because you&#x27;re looking at the real sensor image, there&#x27;s no calibration gap to correct — what you see sharp *is* sharp. It&#x27;s slower and it wants a steady camera, but for adapted vintage glass at f/1.4 it&#x27;s how most people get consistent results. Fine adjustment tightens up the viewfinder-and-confirmation-light workflow; Live View magnification sidesteps the whole issue when you need certainty.<br><br>A third, humble option: **stop down**. At f/2.8 to f/4 the depth of field deepens enough to swallow a small focus error. If the shot allows it, closing the aperture a stop or two is the fastest &quot;fix&quot; of all — no menus, no testing.<br><br>## The short version<br><br>The green dot is a calibrated guess, not a guarantee, and on some camera-and-lens pairings — especially manual lenses on bodies tuned for autofocus — that guess is consistently a few hairs off. AF Fine Adjustment lets you shift the guess back into agreement with reality:<br><br>1. Prove the error is **consistent** and note its **direction** with a 45-degree ruler test, wide open.<br>2. Open the feature, choose **per-lens** if the lens reports electronically, otherwise **global**.<br>3. Nudge **toward +** for front-focus, **toward −** for back-focus, a few steps at a time.<br>4. **Re-test and iterate** until the sharp plane lands where the camera confirmed.<br>5. For manual glass you want perfect wide open, skip all of it and **focus in magnified Live View** — or just **stop down** and let depth of field cover you.<br><br>Done once per troublesome lens, it turns a camera that quietly lies about focus into one you can trust through the viewfinder again.<br></div>
